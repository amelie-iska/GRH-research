Read the model and calibration assumptions

For unit directions nᵢ, the covariance is Cᵢⱼ = exp[(nᵢ·nⱼ − 1)/0.35²] + 0.20² δᵢⱼ. The templates are mean-subtracted angular Gaussians with widths 0.18, 0.30 and 0.45 radians. Filter weights are C⁻¹t / √(tᵀC⁻¹t). A normalized injection is s t / √(tᵀC⁻¹t).

The global statistic is the largest positive matched-filter score across all 288 templates. Its Monte Carlo rank probability is (1 + exceedances) / 4096. When no calibration maximum exceeds the displayed score, 1/4096 is the finite resolution of this run, not a measured tail below that value. The model’s signal units and angular widths are illustrative; they are not a calibrated physical CCC transfer law.

The four recorded examples use one predetermined base sky and seed 20261113. A separate finite null check produced 119 rejections among 2,048 draws at nominal 0.05. This is a conditional diagnostic, not a proof of exact 5% conditional rejection. Interactive injections are hypothetical changes to the data; they supply no new observational evidence.

What the probability theorem assumes

The explicit finite null is Y = Lξ, with independent ideal standard-normal real coordinates ξ. The stored hexadecimal coefficients of L and the filter weights define exact dyadic numbers. Independent integer/Fraction replay verifies every filter variance is at most 1000000000001/1000000000000.

The Gaussian even-moment identity supplies M = (2k−1)!!vk. The Lean theorem then proves the finite-union and moment bound for measurable scores with integrable moments. Correlated filters are allowed. The Gaussian law and moment identity remain explicit model obligations; no property of the fixed pseudorandom output or an observed cosmic sky follows from the arithmetic certificate.

The displayed rank is (1 + stored exceedances)/4096. Its floor 1/4096 is finite calibration resolution. The rank-law/exchangeability theorem is not claimed here, and a single fixed calibration sample does not prove a conditional false-positive rate.

Specify what crosses the channel.

Conformal geometry, physical transfer, an intentional code and a decoder have different mathematical roles. A useful diagram states each role and its assumptions.

Gurzadyan and Penrose proposed searching for cross-aeon communication in 2015. The proposal supplies a research question; it does not report a decoded message. A physical source can leave correlations even when no intentional encoding is posited.

Primary proposal: CCC and the Fermi paradox, §2

Identifiability comes first.

If two messages produce the same observation law, every downstream decoder produces the same output law. With K equally likely, indistinguishable messages, success is at most 1/K.

P(Y | M = m) = P(Y | M = m′)

Computation can improve inference.

A learned model can make existing information easier to use. Under the Markov assumption M → Y → Z, data processing bounds the available information.

I(M; Z) ≤ I(M; Y)

A spinor contraction with explicit hypotheses

Contracting ψABCD with κCD gives φAB. Under the source-free spin-two and symmetric Killing-spinor equations stated in the chapter, φ obeys the Maxwell equation. The corresponding smooth self-dual two-form is closed, so its integrals over homologous surfaces agree by Stokes’ theorem.

The wires here are spinor and dual-spinor indices. This algebraic contraction is not automatically a completely positive, trace-preserving quantum channel.

The 2025 CCC proposal uses a weak-field spinor charge construction and an additional early-aeon isotropy/no-net-flux argument. Meissner–Penrose, §§II–III. The manuscript separates that conditional result from a general nonlinear crossover construction.

Conformal geometry and an elliptic bridge

The chapter proves null-path and Maxwell conformal invariance on regular domains. With conserved radiation, matter and Λ, the Friedmann equation becomes a quartic. Four simple roots give a smooth genus-one curve; its differential gives the conformal-time integral.

That complex analytic integral has a different computational setting from the finite-field discrete logarithm used in ECC.
